\chapter{Miscellaneous Topics}
This chapter covers several miscellaneous topics that bear on the numerical implementation of constitutive models.
\section{Equivalent But Preferred?}
Some (yield) functions are mathematically equivalent, but is there a preferred one, or can any form be used?
Suppose, for example, that we want a yield function enforcing the magnitude of stress $\sigma$ to be less than a yield stress $\sigma_y\geqslant0$.
One can write
\begin{gather}
    f=\abs{\sigma}-\sigma_y,
\end{gather}
or alternatively,
\begin{gather}
    f=\sigma^2-\sigma_y^2.
\end{gather}
They are equivalent, since the yield stress has been defined as non-negative.
Their numerical implications, however, may differ.
First, squares are best avoided: they can easily cause precision issues, especially when the local nonlinear system also contains nondimensional history variables.
Second, if associative flow is used, the above two yield functions lead to
\begin{gather}
    \dot{\varepsilon^p}=\gamma\pdfrac{f}{\sigma}=\left\{\begin{array}{ll}
        \gamma\sign{\sigma}, & \text{for~}f=\abs{\sigma}-\sigma_y, \\[4mm]
        \gamma2\sigma,       & \text{for~}f=\sigma^2-\sigma_y^2.
    \end{array}\right.
\end{gather}
In the first case, $\abs{\dot{\varepsilon^p}}=\gamma$, so $\gamma<\abs{\dot{\varepsilon}}$ provides an explicit bound: the increment of plastic strain can never exceed the increment of total strain.
In the second case, however, the upper bound of $\gamma$ is not easy to determine.
Because the entire plasticity evolution is driven by the plastic multiplier $\gamma$, root-finding algorithms can be used to solve for $\gamma$ whenever an explicit bound is available.
This is particularly useful when the local Newton--Raphson iteration fails to converge.

For numerical robustness, the first form of the yield function is preferred here.
Similar arguments apply to other, more complex formulations.

Can you analyse the pros and cons of the third option shown below?
\begin{gather}
    f=\dfrac{\abs{\sigma}}{\sigma_y}-1.
\end{gather}
\section{First Order or Second Order?}
Let us consider the following ODE,
\begin{gather}
    \dot{y}=a-by,
\end{gather}
with the initial condition $y(0)=0$.
The analytical solution can be easily obtained as
\begin{gather}
    y(t)=\dfrac{a}{b}\left(1-e^{-bt}\right).
\end{gather}

Now let us consider integrating this ODE using the first-order backward Euler method and the second-order Trapezoidal method.
\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{PIC/convergence.integration.pdf}
    \caption{Comparison of first-order backward Euler method and second-order Trapezoidal method for the ODE $\dot{y}=a-by$.}
    \label{fig:convergence}
\end{figure}
\figref{fig:convergence} shows the error in the numerical solutions for $a=1$ and $b=20$.
The system is integrated to $t=10$.
As expected, the Trapezoidal method converges at second order as $\Delta{}t$ decreases, while backward Euler converges at first order.
Truncation error analysis explains this well.

However, for large $\Delta{}t$, the Euler method has the smaller absolute error in the example above.
Even if both methods converge at their nominal orders for arbitrary $\Delta{}t$, a threshold exists beyond which the first-order method yields the smaller absolute error.
After all, ``second-order'' only says that the error is $\mathcal{O}\left(\Delta{}t^2\right)$; it does not mean that a second-order method always yields a smaller absolute error than a first-order one for a given $\Delta{}t$.
The constant hidden in the big-$\mathcal{O}$ notation can be large enough to make the second-order method the worse of the two at a given $\Delta{}t$.

This is not surprising --- two straight lines with different slopes eventually cross each other.
Whether this ``threshold'' is practically relevant has no universal answer.
This subtle implication is thus worth keeping in mind.
When designing a numerical implementation, a second-order method is not necessarily better than a first-order method.
\section{Algorithmic Tangent Stiffness}
In numerical implementations with implicit schemes, the algorithmic tangent stiffness is often needed to preserve the quadratic convergence of the global Newton--Raphson iteration.
Some of the literature calls it the consistent tangent operator.

But what is it exactly?
Let us consider the following ODE as an example,
\begin{gather}
    \ddfrac{y}{x}=y.
\end{gather}
For a function whose derivative is the function itself, we know that the solution is the exponential function,
\begin{gather}
    y=e^x+C
\end{gather}
where $C$ is some constant.

Now suppose we do not know the analytical solution and want to solve this ODE numerically with the backward Euler method.
We have
\begin{gather}
    y_{n+1}-y_n=y\Delta{}x,
\end{gather}
and rearranging the equation gives
\begin{gather}
    y_{n+1}=\dfrac{y_n}{1-\Delta{}x}.
\end{gather}
This is the recursion formula.
Omitting the subscript $\left(\cdot\right)_{n+1}$ for simplicity and differentiating $y$ with respect to $\Delta{}x$ gives
\begin{gather}
    \ddfrac{y}{\Delta{}x}=\dfrac{y_n}{\left(1-\Delta{}x\right)^2}=\dfrac{y}{1-\Delta{}x}.
\end{gather}
On the other hand, because $x=x_n+\Delta{}x$, we have
\begin{gather}
    \md{x}=\md{x_n+\Delta{}x}=\md{\Delta{}x},
\end{gather}
thus the above derivative is
\begin{gather}
    \ddfrac{y}{x}=\dfrac{y}{1-\Delta{}x}.
\end{gather}
Clearly, this differs from the original ODE $\ddfrac{y}{x}=y$.
However, when $\Delta{}x\to0$, the derivative obtained from the discretized equation converges to the original analytical one.

This is the essence of the algorithmic tangent stiffness: the derivative obtained from the discretized equation, \textit{consistent} with the numerical scheme used.
\section{Fancy Solution Techniques}
Some textbooks introduce two general classes of solution techniques (return-mapping algorithms) \cite[see \S~3.6]{Simo1998}: (a) the cutting-plane method and (b) the closest-point projection method, often with geometric interpretations.
This book, however, does not discuss them explicitly.
Familiarity with the algorithms can help, but a detailed grasp of their geometric interpretation is not essential here.
Fundamentally, both are alternative numerical strategies for integrating the constitutive evolution equations.

Specifically, for the local system of ordinary differential equations in \eqref{eq:local_system}, the closest-point projection method is an \textbf{implicit} time integration scheme, whereas the cutting-plane method rests on \textbf{explicit} integration.
Seen this way, the distinction between the two approaches is primarily algorithmic rather than conceptual.
The question is how to discretize the time derivatives in the local evolution equations so that the solution satisfies the yield condition.

As expected, the cutting-plane method inherits the well-known limitations of explicit integration: conditional stability, restrictions on the admissible time-step size, and the accumulation of numerical errors.
For this reason, no cutting-plane-based implementation appears in this book: its applicability is limited in general-purpose computational settings.

More generally, hybrid strategies can be constructed in which different subsets of the constitutive evolution equations are integrated using different numerical schemes, for example by combining implicit and explicit updates within the same local problem.

Finally, it should be noted that some studies have suggested that a consistent algorithmic tangent operator cannot be derived when explicit integration schemes are employed \cite[e.g.,]{Simo1998}.
This claim is not correct \cite{Starman2014}.
The general procedure described in \S~\ref{sec:consistent_tangent_general} remains applicable irrespective of the specific time-integration method used for the constitutive equations.
\section{The Real Safeguard}
Whatever the integration scheme, Newton--Raphson remains the standard way to solve the local nonlinear system, thanks to its quadratic convergence and the consistent tangent operator available from the existing Jacobian.
However, its convergence is strictly local, requiring an initial guess sufficiently close to the solution.

Convergence failures call for a robust algorithm that guarantees successful constitutive integration.
In certain cases the yield function depends solely on the plasticity multiplier, viz., $f=f\left(\gamma\right)$, and root-finding algorithms can then solve for $\gamma$ directly.
For example, Ridders' method is simple and robust: it does not evaluate derivatives, ensures convergence, and can achieve a convergence rate of $\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{2}\right)$ for well-behaved functions.

Consequently, a suitable root-finding algorithm can solve for $\gamma$ within a specified tolerance.
If a strict tolerance is met, the estimate serves as the final solution.
Alternatively, a moderate tolerance provides an initial guess accurate enough for the Newton--Raphson method, ensuring rapid quadratic convergence.
Once the system is solved, the consistent tangent operator follows from standard procedures.

More aggressive implementations may even default to a gradient (Jacobian) free root-finding approach, evaluating the Jacobian only when the consistent tangent operator is needed, after a sufficiently accurate solution is obtained.
Computationally, this is extremely cheap.

In the following, we outline the primary prerequisites for implementing this strategy successfully.
\subsection{A Bounded Multiplier}
Most root-finding algorithms require an initial pair of guesses, denoted $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$ with $\gamma_1<\gamma_2$, that bracket the root, that is, $f\left(\gamma_1\right)\cdot{}f\left(\gamma_2\right)<0$.

For plasticity return-mapping, the solution is guaranteed to exist by construction, and one can always choose $\gamma_1=0$ since it is known that $f\left(0\right)>0$.
The remaining task is to find a suitable $\gamma_2$ such that $f\left(\gamma_2\right)<0$.
Starting from an initial nonzero value of $\gamma_2$, repeatedly double $\gamma_2$ and evaluate $f(\gamma_2)$ until the condition $f(\gamma_2)<0$ is satisfied.

A good guess may come from the flow rule \eqsref{eq:general_flow_rule}, saving unnecessary evaluations of the yield function.
If the flow rule is carefully designed such that $\norm{\mb{r}}\equiv1$, then $\gamma_2=\norm{\dot{\varepsilon}}$ may serve as the initial guess.

For uniaxial models with $\dot{\varepsilon}_p=\pm\gamma$, it can further be shown that $\gamma\leqslant\abs{\dot{\varepsilon}}$.
Choosing $\gamma_2=\abs{\dot{\varepsilon}}$ then ensures the true solution is bracketed.
\subsection{Explicit State Dependency}
The formulation should also make the yield function depend explicitly on the plasticity multiplier $\gamma$: as long as $\gamma$ is known, the yield function can be evaluated without solving any additional nonlinear system.

If this cannot be achieved, a composite implementation that combines global root-finding and local Newton--Raphson methods may be necessary.

In constitutive modelling, analytical formulations must be balanced against numerical considerations that govern implementation robustness and efficiency.
Specifically, explicit rather than implicit evolution laws for internal variables eliminate the auxiliary nonlinear solves typically required to evaluate the yield function.
Explicit rules also decouple the internal variables, which simplifies the local system and streamlines the formulation of the Jacobian matrix.
