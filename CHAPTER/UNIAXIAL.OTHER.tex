\chapter{Uniaxial Plasticity Models (Other Materials)}
\section{K4 Concrete}
We introduce here a uniaxial model for concrete formulated within the classic plasticity framework.
The K4 name comes from the four authors, whose surnames all start with the letter `K'.
The model in the original paper, however, adopts linear degradation, which inevitably drives the stiffness to zero once a certain strain limit is exceeded.
Since numerical models do not handle exact zeros well, we replace the linear degradation with an exponentially decaying function.
Note also that the original formulation \cite{Kenawy2020} contains some mistakes.
Any discrepancy or inconsistency is resolved in favour of the formulation discussed in this section.

Phenomenological models for concrete are numerous.
Because they are phenomenological, different loading and unloading responses can be defined with some flexibility, which makes the models easier for engineers to understand and extrapolate.
They may, however, violate thermodynamic principles.
\subsection{Theory}
The classic plasticity framework offers no way to define stiffness degradation: elastic loading and unloading paths always follow the initial stiffness gradient.
Combining plasticity with damage \cite{Lemaitre1985} is therefore common, so that the apparent stiffness can gradually degrade.

The apparent stress $\sigma$ is conventionally expressed as
\begin{gather}
\sigma=\left(1-d\right)\bar{\sigma},
\end{gather}
where $d$ is the damage factor ranging from zero to unity and $\bar{\sigma}$ is the effective stress.
In this formulation, $d$ accounts for damage and $\bar{\sigma}$ for plasticity.

Just like other plasticity models, the additive decomposition applies such that
\begin{gather}
\bar{\sigma}=E\left(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^p\right).
\end{gather}
\subsubsection{Yield Function}
Unlike metals, concrete does not require ratcheting to be modelled, so only isotropic hardening is conventionally used, with no kinematic hardening; consequently, there is no need to introduce back stress.
The yield function $F$ can be expressed simply as
\begin{gather}
F=\abs{\bar{\sigma}}-\bar{\sigma}_y.
\end{gather}
Accordingly, plastic evolution is triggered whenever the magnitude of the effective stress $\bar{\sigma}$ exceeds the backbone $\bar{\sigma}_y$, which depends on an internal hardening variable.

With only one $\bar{\sigma}_y$, tensile and compressive behaviour cannot be distinguished.
Adopting two sets of rules, one for tension and the other for compression, addresses this.
\begin{gather}
F=\left\{
\begin{array}{ll}
F_t,&\bar{\sigma}>0,\\
F_c,&\text{otherwise},
\end{array}
\right.
\end{gather}
with
\begin{gather}
F_t=\bar{\sigma}-\bar{\sigma}_{y,t},\qquad
F_c=-\bar{\sigma}-\bar{\sigma}_{y,c}.
\end{gather}
\subsubsection{Flow Rule}
For uniaxial models, the flow direction is simply the loading direction,
\begin{gather}
\dot{\varepsilon^p}=\gamma~\sign{\bar{\sigma}}=\gamma\pdfrac{F}{\bar{\sigma}}.
\end{gather}
This coincides with associative plasticity (the second equality).
\subsubsection{Hardening Law}
Any functions can be chosen as the hardening laws, so
\begin{gather}
\bar{\sigma}_{y,t}=H_t\left(k_t\right),\qquad
\bar{\sigma}_{y,c}=H_c\left(k_c\right),
\end{gather}
where $k_t$ and $k_c$ are internal hardening variables.

Because damage theory accounts for stiffness and strength degradation, the hardening functions are often chosen to increase monotonically.

The original formulation adopts the following explicit forms.
\begin{gather}\label{eq:k4_hardening}
\bar{\sigma}_{y,t}=f_t+h_tk_t,\qquad
\bar{\sigma}_{y,c}=\left\{
\begin{array}{ll}
f_y+h_ck_c,&k_c\leqslant{}k_0,\\
f_y+h_ck_0+h_d\left(k_c-k_0\right),&\text{otherwise}.
\end{array}
\right.
\end{gather}
In the above expression, $f_t$ and $f_y$ are the crack strength (for tension, positive) and the yield strength (for compression, positive), $h_t$, $h_c$ and $h_d$ are three positive moduli, and $k_0$ denotes the value of $k_c$ at the compressive crush strength.
In essence, $\bar{\sigma}_{y,t}$ hardens linearly, while $\bar{\sigma}_{y,c}$ hardens bilinearly.
The expression can be simplified further by letting $f_c$ denote $f_y+h_ck_0$.

Since stiffness and strength degradation are handled by the damage part, no softening is considered here, so that
\begin{gather}
h_t,h_c,h_d\geqslant0.
\end{gather}

The evolutions of $k_t$ and $k_c$ are tied to the plastic strain.
\begin{gather}\label{eq:k4_evolution}
\dot{k}_t=\abs{\dot{\varepsilon^p}}=\gamma\qquad\text{for tension loading},\\
\dot{k}_c=\abs{\dot{\varepsilon^p}}=\gamma\qquad\text{for compression loading}.
\end{gather}
Although both share the same expression, only one of the two yield functions is activated in a single loading step.
Hence the plastic strain increment $\gamma$ for that step can contribute to either $k_t$ or $k_c$, not both.
\subsubsection{Degradation}
The yield function, flow rule, and hardening law above effectively define a very simple plasticity model with only isotropic hardening, although it may behave differently in tension and compression.
It resembles the model introduced in \secref{sec:uniaxial_brb}, which uses the same concept to distinguish tension from compression but with more complex specific functions.

Similarly, it is desirable to control the degradation rate independently for tension and compression.
To this end,
\begin{gather}
d=\left\{
\begin{array}{ll}
d_t,&\bar{\sigma}>0,\\
d_c,&\text{otherwise},
\end{array}
\right.
\end{gather}
The specific forms of tensile damage $d_t$ and compressive damage $d_c$ can vary: the original formulation \cite{Kenawy2020}, for instance, uses the linear degradation of \cite{Grassl2013}.
Here, we use a simpler exponential function.
\begin{gather}
d_t=1-\exp\left(-\dfrac{k_t}{e_{r,t}}\right),\qquad
d_c=1-\exp\left(-\dfrac{k_c}{e_{r,c}}\right).
\end{gather}
The parameters $e_{r,t}$ and $e_{r,c}$ are introduced to control the degradation rate.

In compression, when the original bilinear hardening function \eqsref{eq:k4_hardening} is used, the crush strength should still be reached, so damage evolution should be delayed.
The following function
\begin{gather*}
d_c=1-\exp\left(-\dfrac{\max\left(k_c-k_0,0\right)}{e_{r,c}}\right)
\end{gather*}
can be used so that $d_c$ stays at zero until $k_c$ becomes larger than $k_0$.
\paragraph{Total Energy}
If linear hardening is used, for example,
\begin{gather}
\bar{\sigma}_y=ak+b.
\end{gather}
Here we do not distinguish between tension and compression, and we use the general parameters $a$ and $b$.
Combining $\bar{\sigma}_y$ with the damage factor gives the final stress backbone as
\begin{gather}
\sigma_y=\left(1-d\right)\bar{\sigma}_y=\exp\left(-\dfrac{k}{c}\right)\left(ak+b\right).
\end{gather}
Integration leads to
\begin{gather}
\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma_y~\md{k}=ac^2+bc.
\end{gather}

The initial slope is
\begin{gather}
\ddfrac{\sigma_y}{k}\bigg|_{k=0}=a-\dfrac{b}{c}.
\end{gather}
For this to be less than zero (no initial hardening, softening only), we require
\begin{gather}
ac<b.
\end{gather}
If $a=0$, there is no initial hardening whatever the value of $c$, since $b>0$.
If $ac\geqslant{}b$, implying $a>0$, the backbone has a peak value at $k=c-\dfrac{b}{a}$.

We can associate $c$ with $b$ by setting $c=\dfrac{b}{\zeta{}E}$, where $\zeta$ is a positive multiplier.
The total energy then becomes
\begin{gather}
a\dfrac{b^2}{\zeta^2E^2}+b\dfrac{b}{\zeta{}E}=\left(\dfrac{a}{\zeta^2E^2}+\dfrac{1}{\zeta{}E}\right)b^2.
\end{gather}
The peak value for $\zeta{}E<a$ is
\begin{gather}
\dfrac{ab}{\zeta{}E}\exp\left(\dfrac{\zeta{}E-a}{a}\right).
\end{gather}

Given the reference $\zeta_r$ for the reference characteristic length $l_r$, for any characteristic length $l$, if the total energy is to remain unchanged,
\begin{gather}
\left(\dfrac{a}{\zeta^2E^2}+\dfrac{1}{\zeta{}E}\right)b^2l=\left(\dfrac{a}{\zeta_r^2E^2}+\dfrac{1}{\zeta_r{}E}\right)b^2l_r,\\
\dfrac{a}{\zeta^2E^2}+\dfrac{1}{\zeta{}E}=\dfrac{l_r}{l}\left(\dfrac{a}{\zeta_r^2E^2}+\dfrac{1}{\zeta_r{}E}\right),\\
\dfrac{1}{\zeta{}E}=\dfrac{\sqrt{1+4\dfrac{l_r}{l}\left(\dfrac{a^2}{\zeta_r^2E^2}+\dfrac{a}{\zeta_r{}E}\right)}-1}{2a}.
\end{gather}
Denoting $r_a=\dfrac{a}{E}$,
\begin{gather}
\zeta=\dfrac{2r_a}{\sqrt{1+4\dfrac{l_r}{l}\left(\dfrac{r_a^2}{\zeta_r^2}+\dfrac{r_a}{\zeta_r}\right)}-1}.
\end{gather}

Note that here we enforce only constancy of the total energy.
This is not equivalent to obtaining objective results under arbitrary loading magnitudes.
\subsubsection{Crack/Gap Closing}
According to \eqsref{eq:k4_evolution}, $k_t$ is the accumulated tensile plastic strain, which physically represents the crack opening.
When reloading towards compression, this opening can be gradually closed.

To account for this crack-closing mechanism, an additional history variable $k_k$ is introduced to allow further plasticity to develop; it tracks crack closing.

For this inner plasticity, we adopt a simple linear rule such that a fixed fraction of the strain increment contributes to plastic strain, that is
\begin{gather}
\Delta\varepsilon^p=\Delta\varepsilon\dfrac{E}{E+h_k}.
\end{gather}

Given that this is only activated during compressive loading ($\bar\sigma<0$ and $\Delta\varepsilon<0$), we have $\Delta\varepsilon^p<0$ and $\abs{\Delta\varepsilon^p}=-\Delta\varepsilon^p$.
\begin{gather}
\Delta{}k_k=-\Delta\varepsilon^p,\\
\Delta\bar{\sigma}=-E\Delta\varepsilon^p.
\end{gather}

This mechanism states that, during reloading towards compression ($\bar\sigma<0$ and $\Delta\varepsilon<0$), whenever $k_k<k_t$, that is, crack closing is smaller than crack opening, there is still net crack opening that needs to be closed.
Under this condition, the inner plasticity is activated so that part of the strain increment is converted to plastic strain (crack closing).
The following two implementation details need to be considered to ensure a correct model.
\paragraph{Entering}
The exact transition step from tensile to compressive stress needs special treatment: the tensile stress must fully unload to zero, which involves no plasticity, and the remainder is compressive loading, which does involve plasticity.
Thus $\Delta\varepsilon^p$ should be limited such that
\begin{gather}\label{eq:k4_closing_strain}
\Delta\varepsilon^p=\left(\Delta\varepsilon+\dfrac{\max\left(\bar\sigma_n,0\right)}{E}\right)\dfrac{E}{E+h_k}=\Delta\varepsilon\dfrac{E}{E+h_k}+\dfrac{\max\left(\bar\sigma_n,0\right)}{E+h_k}.
\end{gather}
The above expression states that if the current effective stress $\bar{\sigma}_n$ is positive (tensile), then we remove the corresponding tensile strain ($\dfrac{\max\left(\bar\sigma_n,0\right)}{E}$) from the total strain increment $\Delta\varepsilon$.
The resulting $\Delta\varepsilon^p$ is therefore induced entirely by the compressive part.
\paragraph{Exiting}
Similarly, the exact transition step from not-yet-closed to fully closed also needs additional attention.
Since $k_k$ should never exceed $k_t$, and $k_t$ does not change during the evolution of $k_k$, we have
\begin{gather}
k_k=k_{k,n}+\Delta{}k_k=k_{k,n}-\Delta\varepsilon^p\leqslant{}k_t,
\end{gather}
equivalently,
\begin{gather}
k_{k,n}-k_t\leqslant{}\Delta\varepsilon^p.
\end{gather}
If the above condition is not satisfied by the plastic strain increment computed from \eqsref{eq:k4_closing_strain}, then we should manually set $\Delta\varepsilon^p=k_{k,n}-k_t$ and update $k_k$ and $\bar{\sigma}$ accordingly.
\paragraph{Remark}
For crack closing, the original model \cite{Kenawy2020} formulates it as a plasticity model but does not use a return-mapping algorithm in the corresponding implementation.
Characterising such a mechanism using a yield function, such as
\begin{gather*}
F_k=\abs{\bar{\sigma}}-\bar{\sigma}_k
\end{gather*}
with $\bar{\sigma}_k=h_kk_k$, does not properly define the behaviour.
Especially under cyclic loading, the elastic region grows whenever $\bar{\sigma}_k$ grows.

To correct the response, however, the mechanism has to be defined at least as
\begin{gather}
\bar{\sigma}_k=h_k\left(k_k-k_k^i\right),
\end{gather}
where $k_k^i$ is the initial $k_k$ at the beginning of the current loading path, which needs updating whenever load reversal occurs.

We avoid such a complex presentation because it may cause unnecessary confusion.
At its core, the crack-closing mechanism tries to close the unclosed crack ($k_t-k_k$) by accumulating a portion of the strain increment into $k_k$ ($\Delta{}k_k=\abs{\Delta\varepsilon}\dfrac{E}{E+h_k}$), and this mechanism is activated only under certain conditions.
\subsection{Formulation}
\subsubsection{Tangent Stiffness}
By the chain rule, differentiating the expression of stress
\begin{gather}
\sigma=\left(1-d\right)\bar{\sigma}
\end{gather}
leads to
\begin{gather}
\ddfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}=\left(1-d\right)\ddfrac{\bar{\sigma}}{\varepsilon}-\bar{\sigma}\ddfrac{d}{k}\ddfrac{k}{\varepsilon}.
\end{gather}
Here we use an `anisotropic' damage concept, that is
\begin{gather}
d=\left\{
\begin{array}{ll}
d_t,&\bar{\sigma}>0,\\
d_c,&\text{else},
\end{array}\right.\qquad
k=\left\{
\begin{array}{ll}
k_t,&\bar{\sigma}>0,\\
k_c,&\text{else}.
\end{array}\right.
\end{gather}

Knowing that $\ddfrac{k}{\varepsilon}$ is effectively $\ddfrac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}$ as $\dot{k}=\gamma$, from the local residual (yield function) at equilibrium,
\begin{gather}
\pdfrac{F}{\varepsilon}=\pdfrac{F}{\gamma}\ddfrac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}+\pdfrac{F}{\varepsilon}=0,
\end{gather}
one could derive
\begin{gather}
\ddfrac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}=-\left(\pdfrac{F}{\gamma}\right)^{-1}\pdfrac{F}{\varepsilon}.
\end{gather}

The yield function can be expressed as
\begin{gather}
\begin{split}
F&=\abs{\bar{\sigma}}-\bar{\sigma}_y\\
&=\abs{\bar{\sigma}^\text{trial}-\gamma{}E\sign{\bar{\sigma}}}-\bar{\sigma}_y,
\end{split}
\end{gather}
then
\begin{gather}
\pdfrac{F}{\gamma}=-\sign{\bar{\sigma}}E\sign{\bar{\sigma}}-\ddfrac{\bar{\sigma}_y}{k}=-E-\ddfrac{\bar{\sigma}_y}{k},\\
\pdfrac{F}{\varepsilon}=\sign{\bar{\sigma}}E.
\end{gather}
In the above, $J=\pdfrac{F}{\gamma}$ is in fact the Jacobian of the local system.
Thus,
\begin{gather}
\ddfrac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}=-\sign{\bar{\sigma}}\dfrac{E}{J}.
\end{gather}

For the effective stress,
\begin{gather}
\ddfrac{\bar{\sigma}}{\varepsilon}=E-\sign{\bar{\sigma}}E\ddfrac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}=E+\dfrac{E^2}{J}.
\end{gather}

The stiffness can be expressed as
\begin{gather}
\ddfrac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}=\left(1-d\right)\left(E+\dfrac{E^2}{J}\right)+\abs{\bar{\sigma}}\ddfrac{d}{k}\dfrac{E}{J}.
\end{gather}
The damage evolution determines the explicit form of $\ddfrac{d}{k}$.
\subsection{Implementation}
\begin{breakablealgorithm}
\caption{state determination of K4 concrete model}\label{algo:k4_concrete}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State compute trial stress
\If{crack closing is activated}
\State apply crack closing plasticity
\EndIf
\If{tension}
\State apply tensile plasticity
\State apply tensile damage
\Else
\State apply compressive plasticity
\State apply compressive damage
\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}
